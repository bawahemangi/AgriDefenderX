# Tomato — Early Blight (Alternaria solani)

## Overview
Fungal disease causing dark concentric-ring spots on older, lower leaves first, which enlarge and cause yellowing and leaf drop. Can spread to stems and fruit in humid conditions.

## Favorable conditions
Warm temperatures (24-29°C), high humidity or leaf wetness, dense canopy with poor air circulation, and plant stress (nutrient deficiency, water stress).

## Integrated pest & disease management actions
- Remove and destroy infected lower leaves as soon as spots appear; do not compost them on-farm.
- Improve airflow: avoid overcrowding, stake/prune plants to open the canopy.
- Avoid overhead irrigation; water at the base in the morning so foliage dries during the day.
- Practice crop rotation (avoid planting tomato/potato in the same plot for 2 years).
- Mulch around the base to reduce soil splash onto lower leaves, which is the main infection pathway.
- Ensure balanced nutrition — nitrogen-stressed and potassium-deficient plants are more susceptible.

## Safe input use
Copper-based or approved fungicide sprays can help protect healthy tissue when disease pressure is high, applied preventively or at first symptom appearance. Always use a product registered for tomato early blight in your state, follow the label rate exactly, respect the pre-harvest interval, and rotate fungicide groups (FRAC codes) to avoid resistance build-up. Do not increase dose beyond the label to "be safe" — this raises residue risk without improving control.

## When to refer to an expert or lab
If spots appear on more than 25-30% of the plant, if fruit lesions appear, or if the diagnosis is uncertain (early blight, septoria leaf spot, and bacterial spot can look similar), refer to the local agriculture extension officer or a plant pathology lab for confirmation before large-scale spraying.

## Follow-up monitoring
Re-inspect the field every 3-5 days during humid weather. Track whether the affected leaf fraction is growing or stabilizing after sanitation/spray actions.
